Abstract

Learning from human demonstrations has enabled robots to acquire a wide range of manipulation skills, but learned policies typically execute far slower than ordinary humans. This speed gap is mainly due to lack of an interface for collecting demonstration data at high speed, and the difficulty in training policies that can robustly execute high-speed motions. In this paper, we present ALOHA Lightning, a system for learning fast and precise robotic manipulation. Our system uses kinesthetic teaching to intuitively collect near-human-speed demonstrations on a backdrivable bimanual platform, yielding natural, fast, and joint-accurate trajectories. The core insight is that speed depends on the ability of the system to capture real-time data with high fidelity. We also present a learning pipeline that enables smooth high-speed execution through test-time action smoothing and aligns the visual data distribution between data collection and deployment with masking. Given 50 demonstrations for each task, ALOHA Lightning autonomously completes bimanual tasks such as ball tossing and catching, folding shorts, battery insertion, and bussing tables for over 80% success rates while close to human speed.

Masking

During data collection, the operator's hands and arms are in view of the cameras; at deployment the robot is alone. Using forward kinematics and the calibrated transform between the arms, we compute where the handles appear in every camera and mask those regions in real time, both in training and at deployment, so the policy sees the same visual distribution in both.

Smoothing

At high speed, the jumps between consecutive action chunks become large enough to destabilize the robot. Each new chunk is predicted H steps before the previous one runs out, and over that overlap we blend the two with a sigmoid weight. Temporal ensembling (from ACT) queries the policy at every timestep, so every action is covered by many overlapping chunks. The executed action at is a weighted average of all of their predictions for timestep t, where â(i)t is the i-th prediction, counting from the oldest, and m sets how quickly newer predictions are folded in. The result is smooth, but older predictions keep pulling on every action, and in our experiments policies with temporal ensembling executed at roughly half the speed. Our method only blends across the short overlap between two consecutive chunks.
